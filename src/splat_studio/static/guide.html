Recording guide

Splat Studio can only rebuild what the camera saw clearly. Film the object from every side, in one continuous take.

Avoid
  • Glass, mirrors, chrome and glossy paint
  • Plain white tables and blank walls
  • Turntables, because the background has to stay still
  • People, pets or curtains moving in view
  • Cuts, fast pans and zooming
  • A large open room behind the object, which turns into fog

1Pick the spot

  • Leave room to walk all the way around the object, a steady 1 to 2 m from its surface.
  • Put it on a textured surface such as a rug, newspaper or patterned cloth. COLMAP needs texture to track the camera.
  • Keep the background close and simple. Distant surroundings that are only filmed briefly become fog around the model.
  • Matte objects work best. Cover or skip reflective and transparent parts.

2Get the light right

  • Soft, even light, like an overcast day or a bright room with several lamps.
  • Avoid harsh sun and single spotlights. Hard shadows and highlights shift as you move.
  • Don't change the lighting during the take, and keep your shadow off the object.

3Set up the phone

  • Record 4K at 30 fps in landscape. Stay on the 1× lens and don't zoom, or the phone may switch cameras.
  • Lock focus and exposure: press and hold on the object until "AE/AF Lock" appears.
  • Turn off Cinematic and Action mode. HDR is converted automatically, but it's better off.

4Record

  • One continuous take with three loops, as in the diagram. End each loop where it started, then rise gently into the next.
  • Why one take: Splat Studio follows the camera from each frame to the next, so one video links every loop to the others. Separate clips would have to be stitched together by matching alone, which is slower and fails more often.
  • Walk slowly, about one step every 2 to 3 seconds. Turn your whole body at corners instead of swinging the phone.
  • Keep about the same distance from the object's surface, not from its centre: around a long object, walk a rounded rectangle rather than a circle.
  • Keep the object in frame, filling half to two-thirds of it. If it's too big to fit (a car up close), filming part of it is fine as long as you move slowly.
  • Total length: 1½ to 4 minutes.

5Check the footage

  • Scrub through the video. Every moment should be sharp, with no jumps, flicker or people walking through.
  • After a run, Splat Studio shows at which seconds the camera tracking broke, so you know what to film again.

6More tips

  • Move smoothly, as if the phone were on rails: steady speed, no sudden turns. Each frame should show most of what the previous one showed.
  • Wipe the lens first. A fingerprint smear softens every frame.
  • Keep windows and bright lamps behind you, not behind the object: backlight darkens it and causes flare.
  • Small objects: raise them on a box under a patterned cloth, so the low loop can look slightly up at them.
  • For details that matter (a logo, a wheel, a face), finish with a slow close pass in the same take: move in gradually, then back out. Never jump closer.
  • Plain surroundings? Lay a few magazines or a patterned cloth around the object to give the tracking something to hold on to.
  • Outdoors, film on an overcast day or in open shade, and be quick: passing clouds change the light during the take.
  • Near shiny objects, wear plain, dark clothes: your reflection moves with you.

7Cars and other large objects

  • Walk a rounded rectangle that follows the body 1.5 to 2 m from the paint, not a circle around the car's centre. Coverage is measured in angles, so the shape of your walk doesn't count against you.
  • Three loops: crouched at wheel height, at eye level, then as high as you can (arms raised, a step stool or a selfie stick) to see the roof.
  • Park with about 2 m free all around, away from other cars, on dry asphalt or paving. A side against a wall is recognised and won't stop the run, but it can't be rebuilt.
  • Paint, windows and chrome act like mirrors: overcast light helps more than anything else. Expect the windows to come out hazy.
  • Allow 3 to 5 minutes, and raise Frames (New run → Advanced) to about 400 so every part gets enough views.
  • The underside can't be filmed and never counts as missing. Wheels need a slow close pass in the same take; the interior seen through the windows usually stays soft.

8People

  • Ask first. A 3D model of someone is personal: get their OK before filming and before sharing it.
  • The person has to stay completely still, which is the hard part. Sitting is far easier than standing; rest the hands on the knees or an armrest instead of holding them up.
  • Give them a point at eye height to look at, and a relaxed expression they can hold. Blinking is fine; moving the eyes or the head isn't.
  • Keep it short, 45 to 90 seconds: two loops for someone seated (chest height, then just above the head), plus a low loop for a full standing body.
  • Move steadily, a little faster than for an object: less time means less movement. Never turn the person on a swivel chair; the background must stay still around them.
  • Matte, patterned clothes help; plain black, plain white, shiny or sequinned fabric is hard. Take off glasses and tie back loose hair: flyaway strands come out blurry.
  • Use soft light from several sides, so the face has no hard shadows.
  • Choose What to keep → One object to keep only the person and drop the chair.
